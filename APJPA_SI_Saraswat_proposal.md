# Inward and outward accountability for government AI use: executive instruments in five Asia-Pacific jurisdictions

Proposal for the special issue "Governing Artificial Intelligence in the Public Sector: Comparative Pathways in the Asia-Pacific and Beyond", *Asia Pacific Journal of Public Administration*

## Research Objectives and Questions

Governments have put AI inside their own administrations faster than they have settled how that use is to be answered for. Accountability scholarship presumes an actor who explains conduct to a forum with standing to question and judge (Bovens, 2007). Where a ministry deploys AI, writes the rules for the deployment and hears the account, that separation blurs, and the instruments governing state AI use say "accountability" about two different relationships. In one, officials answer to bodies inside the executive: an accountable officer, a governance board, a central agency. In the other, the administration answers to forums outside it, among them the people a system affects.

Kumar and Sahu (2026) compare AI governance across regions in 24 national strategies, laws and ethics frameworks, and name public administration among settings for future research. Their documents address society at large rather than governments as users of AI. This study extends their comparative logic to the executive instruments that Australia, India, Japan, Singapore and Thailand have issued for their own AI use.

The study has two objectives. It assembles a corpus of those instruments. It then establishes, through structured coding, how accountability divides between inward and outward forums and how binding each provision is.

RQ1. How are public-sector AI governance arrangements configured across the five jurisdictions, relative to EU and US comparators?

RQ2. How is accountability institutionalised through inward and outward mechanisms in these arrangements?

RQ3. How far do these configurations reflect or depart from patterns identified by Kumar and Sahu (2026)?

## Comparative Framework / Methodology

Case selection varies instrument form while holding the object constant. Australia's policy binds its agencies, and Japan's guideline presents itself as normative for ministries. The Indian, Singaporean and Thai instruments are guidance. Administrative structure also varies, from federal to unitary and from centralised to siloed.

The corpus holds 11 executive instruments issued between 2018 and 15 September 2026: central policy, agency guidance, technical standards and playbooks. Courts and legislatures fall outside it. Four reference frameworks, the EU AI Act, US OMB Memorandum M-25-21, the OECD Recommendation and the UNESCO Recommendation, are coded on the same scheme, limited to provisions on public authorities' own AI use, and kept outside country values.

The unit of coding is the provision. Each provision is assigned to an indicator and scored on a Governance Obligation Level: 0, no qualifying provision; 1, principle; 2, specific non-binding measure; 3, binding duty. A "must" inside a voluntary instrument scores 2. Indicators fall into five groups: inward accountability, outward accountability, human oversight, transparency and fairness. They draw on accountability theory, Kumar and Sahu's Table A1 and the reference frameworks, and are fixed after a pilot on contrasting instruments. Privacy is outside the scope.

Figure 1 plots each country's highest level on five spokes, one panel per country, with the reference frameworks as outlines. A ceiling cannot show how widely a level is reached, so a companion table reports how many indicators reach each level. Every value is tied to an instrument and page. Singapore's operative rules are non-public circulars, and its panel will say so.

One analyst codes the corpus and repeats the pass on a subsample to test intra-coder stability. Where no official English text exists, coding uses machine translation with original pagination retained. Translation can soften mandatory wording, so levels may understate binding force.

## Theoretical Contribution

| | Inward | Outward |
|---|---|---|
| Forum receiving the account | Officers, boards and central agencies inside the executive | Forums outside the executive, such as affected persons and the public |
| Function | Control over officials and systems | Standing to understand and contest |
| Typical mechanisms | Responsible officer, register, impact assessment, reporting upward | Reasons for decisions, contestability, remedy, complaints channel |
| Coded indicators | Seven | Five |
| Basis of comparison | Forums can be compared by position inside or outside the executive. | Forums can be compared by position inside or outside the executive. |

Qiu and Lee (2026) link vertical and horizontal accountability to transparency. This study instead classifies the forums to which government answers. Bovens (2007) sorts forums by kind; the classification here uses one criterion, position relative to the executive hierarchy, so that each indicator can be assigned to a side, scored and compared across jurisdictions.

The distinction earns its place only if the two sides differ in obligation level or breadth. If inward and outward indicators reach the same levels equally widely, it fails, and the paper reports that.

## Expected Findings

Full coding has not begun. A pilot on Australian, Indian and Japanese instruments, run before the codebook was fixed, suggests four propositions.

Inward mechanisms will reach binding levels more widely than outward ones. Australia's instruments record binding duties across nearly all inward indicators but, among the outward ones, only a complaints channel and public justification.

Contestability and remedy will be the thinnest provisions. In the pilot they appear at most as principles, and only in the Indian framework.

Kumar and Sahu's regime labels, drawn from society-wide documents, may fit poorly inside government. Australia and Japan appear to bind their own officials more tightly than a "hybrid" label implies, which RQ3 tests directly.

Singapore will probably register little, because its operative rules are not public. The figure will mark that as a limit of access, not read it as an absence of governance.
